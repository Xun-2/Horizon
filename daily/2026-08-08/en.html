TutorMoments Tests When AI Tutors Should Hold Back

Effective tutoring requires judging whether a student needs scaffolding—support that makes a problem accessible—or enough space and challenge to reason independently. The authors argue that existing tutor benchmarks miss this context-sensitive trade-off, while LLMs’ default helpfulness can eliminate the productive struggle that supports learning.

Background

Effective tutoring requires judging whether a student needs scaffolding—support that makes a problem accessible—or enough space and challenge to reason independently. The authors argue that existing tutor benchmarks miss this context-sensitive trade-off, while LLMs’ default helpfulness can eliminate the productive struggle that supports learning.

Solution

TutorMoments replays decision points from 462 de-identified transcripts of one-to-one U.S. math tutoring for grades 2–7, using more than 1,500 key moments and several thousand annotations supplied by 27 experienced teachers. At each majority-labeled moment, a candidate model takes over for five turns with an LLM-simulated student; a separate model classifier, validated against teacher annotations, scores whether the tutor appropriately scaffolded, pushed for rigor, or avoided excessive support. Across seven LLMs, a prompt that explicitly described these choices improved every model over a generic instruction to “tutor well,” yet results varied widely and models still favored over-helping, used fewer rigor strategies than humans, and rarely stepped back to let students work. The released preview includes transcripts, replay code, and generated continuations, but its scores measure behavior rather than learning: the student is simulated, rigor detection is noisier, and the dataset is limited to a narrow U.S. math setting; moreover, human scores are not a performance ceiling because annotators deliberately selected missed opportunities.

Takeaway

The authors’ central claim is that evaluating an AI tutor requires testing whether its intervention fits the learner’s immediate needs, not merely whether it gives hints or withholds answers. TutorMoments offers a reproducible early framework for that judgment, while leaving real-student learning outcomes as the necessary next test.
