Background

This can provide large model capacity while reducing the computation required for individual requests, although deployment still depends on the full system's memory and infrastructure requirements.

Block Pruning as an Ising Optimization Problem

Most block-removal methods rank transformer blocks independently using magnitude, sensitivity, or influence scores, or restrict the search to one consecutive span. The authors argue that this misses interactions: whether removing one block is harmful can depend on which neighboring or distant blocks are removed, especially under aggressive compression.

Details and sources

The authors argue that this misses interactions: whether removing one block is harmful can depend on which neighboring or distant blocks are removed, especially under aggressive compression.

Background

Most block-removal methods rank transformer blocks independently using magnitude, sensitivity, or influence scores, or restrict the search to one consecutive span. The authors argue that this misses interactions: whether removing one block is harmful can depend on which neighboring or distant blocks are removed, especially under aggressive compression.

Solution

They assign each block a binary variable and use a second-order Taylor expansion of loss to construct a Hessian-like matrix, whose diagonal represents individual importance and whose off-diagonal terms capture pairwise couplings. Selecting exactly M blocks to remove then becomes a constrained binary optimization, or equivalently an Ising-glass problem, with energy xᵀH⁰x; the matrix is computed once from forward and backward passes on a small calibration set, after which candidate configurations can be ranked cheaply and the same matrix reused for different compression targets. Small instances can be exhaustively enumerated—removing 8 of Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct’s 80 blocks involved roughly 29 billion configurations and took about two days on one GPU—while larger problems can use tabu search and other QUBO or Ising solvers. Because energy is only a proxy for benchmark quality, the authors examine several low-energy excited states rather than trusting only the ground state: on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, the 17th excited state proposed an early-layer removal that outperformed the ground-state configuration after light retraining. Across the reported tests, gains increased with compression; without retraining, removing 40 of 80 blocks from Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct yielded 76.9 MMLU versus 54.0 for block influence, while the approach also improved results on Qwen3-14B and heterogeneous Nemotron configurations.

Takeaway

The article’s central claim is that modeling block interactions turns pruning from independent scoring into a tractable search over coupled configurations, with the largest reported benefits in deep compression. Its results suggest that low-energy candidate sets—and not necessarily a single optimum—can expose useful pruning patterns across both homogeneous and heterogeneous architectures, although the evidence is presented primarily through the authors’ reported experiments.
