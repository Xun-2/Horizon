Background

Decision models are designed to take a state and a typed schema as input and return structured decisions, rather than generate open-ended text. Cloudflare’s Clef and Clef-flash are open-weight models hosted through Workers AI, accompanied by a reinforcement-learning fine-tuning service for adapting such models to specific tasks.

Impact

Developers evaluating Clef for moderation or other high-volume decisions must weigh its reported latency, moderation quality, and token pricing against alternatives such as Jev, while also accounting for the limits of an open-weight rather than fully reproducible open-source release.

Community Discussion

Commenters disagreed about Clef's practical value: one reported that it was 2–3 times slower and caught less hate speech than Jev in a moderation workflow, while others noted that Clef costs about $0.24 per million input tokens versus Jev's $0.042 and that Clef-flash is priced at $0.09. Several commenters also stressed that permissively licensed weights do not make the models fully open source because the data and training pipeline remain unpublished.

Background

Approximate nearest-neighbor (ANN) indexes accelerate similarity search by trading exactness for speed. Treating ANN as a secondary index separates the search structure from primary row storage, allowing the index to be rebuilt or changed without relocating the underlying data; Turbopuffer described its storage-architecture change on September 30, 2026.

Impact

Developers building AI retrieval infrastructure may have more flexibility to update and reorganize stored records without rebuilding the primary ANN-oriented layout, although the practical benefit remains unverified without measurements.

Community Discussion

Commenters generally supported treating ANN as a secondary index, citing parallels with PostgreSQL and MySQL, LanceDB's fragment-based design, and SQLite-based multi-database systems. They also noted that vector databases have primarily served retrieval workloads and that the surrounding AI infrastructure market has experienced pronounced cycles, while offering no consensus on comparative performance.

Olmo-core 3 Scales Open MoE Training

Mixture-of-experts models increase total parameter capacity while activating only a subset of specialized components for each token, but storing experts and routing data across GPU clusters can erase those computational savings. Allen AI presents Olmo-core 3 as an open training stack designed to reduce these memory, communication, and coordination costs at trillion-parameter scale.

Details and sources

Allen AI presents Olmo-core 3 as an open training stack designed to reduce these memory, communication, and coordination costs at trillion-parameter scale.

Background

Mixture-of-experts models increase total parameter capacity while activating only a subset of specialized components for each token, but storing experts and routing data across GPU clusters can erase those computational savings. Allen AI presents Olmo-core 3 as an open training stack designed to reduce these memory, communication, and coordination costs at trillion-parameter scale.

Solution

The redesign replaces the earlier FSDP approach, which repeatedly gathered and resharded weights, with DDP that keeps experts resident on GPUs and sends tokens to them; in a preliminary eight-B300 test, this raised throughput for a 47-billion-parameter MoE from 19,400 to 52,000 tokens per second per GPU. Expert and pipeline parallelism divide experts and layers across devices, while a distributed optimizer shards training state; rowwise expert parallelism, GPU-resident routing metadata, and grouped GEMM further reduce routing and computation overhead. In another benchmark, expanding from 8 to 128 experts while selecting four per token increased total capacity from 4.6B to 47B parameters with less than a 5% throughput loss, and MXFP8 improved throughput by about 21% over BF16 while reducing peak active memory from 103 GiB to 95 GiB. The authors also report scaling tests up to 1.2 trillion parameters across 512 B300 GPUs, while stressing that random routing measured systems performance rather than model quality and that a 2.38-trillion-parameter DeepEP v2 experiment was only a short capacity test. Their experiments expose subtler pitfalls: routing scores can hide workload imbalance, identical matrix shapes can run differently with different values, and overlapping communication with computation can sometimes reduce end-to-end throughput.

Takeaway

The authors argue that open model development should include the infrastructure and rejected design choices behind the weights, because scalable MoE training depends on coordinating memory, routing, computation, and communication rather than optimizing any component in isolation.
