Background

A mixture-of-experts model contains many total parameters but activates only a smaller subset for each input, which can reduce the compute required per request. “Sovereign” AI generally refers to systems that organizations can deploy and control within their own jurisdiction or infrastructure; Kolibri’s Apache 2.0 open-weight license and English-German design support that deployment model.

Impact

Developers and organizations can inspect and experiment with an open-weight model designed for controlled deployment and abstention, although its real-world performance and adoption remain unconfirmed.

Others highlighted the sovereignty focus and shared a temporary free hosted trial, but the positive performance assessment included a disclosure from a member of the training team.

It presents enforceable limits as an important safety and cost-control feature for cloud and AI-related services. The discussion highlights debate over the technical feasibility of stopping usage and billing reliably, while noting that current support remains limited rather than broadly available.

Background

A hard budget cap is an enforceable spending limit that automatically stops usage once a preset threshold is reached, unlike alerts or estimates that merely notify users after costs accumulate. Cloud providers have traditionally relied on monitoring, billing alerts, and account controls, but the article argues that these safeguards should become default settings, with unrestricted spending requiring explicit opt-in.

Impact

Cloud and AI users remain exposed to runaway charges when providers lack broad, enforceable caps, and existing controls may be constrained by limited service coverage or billing-period rules.

Community Discussion

Commenters broadly support hard spending caps but are frustrated that major providers appear to offer them only recently and, in at least one reported case, for just a few services with monthly limits. The discussion is divided over whether the delay reflects technical difficulty or business incentives, while others identify prepaid services as an existing alternative.

ThinkingBox Tests Whether Agents Actually Finish the Job

AI agents can produce convincing replies and valid tool-call traces while leaving business records incorrect, incomplete, or burdened with unintended side effects. ThinkingBox addresses this gap by judging the terminal backend state: in one example, an agent correctly reviewed a delayed $745 order but wrongly closed the support ticket instead of placing it on hold.

Background

AI agents can produce convincing replies and valid tool-call traces while leaving business records incorrect, incomplete, or burdened with unintended side effects. ThinkingBox addresses this gap by judging the terminal backend state: in one example, an agent correctly reviewed a delayed $745 order but wrongly closed the support ticket instead of placing it on hold.

Solution

The benchmark runs 507 synthetic business workflows 20 times each from identical, isolated states, reporting average success, whether a task ever succeeds, and whether it succeeds in all 20 attempts. The authors found that one-shot capability often concealed severe inconsistency: Kimi-K3 solved 93.89% of tasks at least once but only 13.41% every time, whereas Claude Opus 5 achieved 20/20 success on 47.53% despite narrower coverage. Among 79,853 failed attempts in a common-set ablation, 67.24% ended cleanly after invoking a state-changing tool, demonstrating that successful-looking trajectories are weak evidence of correct outcomes. Failure labels attributed 79.9% of failures to tool handling, suggesting that retries, precondition handling, and recovery from empty or failed lookups may matter more than additional reasoning alone. Cost rankings also changed with the objective: GPT-5.6 Sol was cheapest per single successful attempt, while GPT-5.4 was cheapest per dependable task, though its 20/20 coverage was lower than GPT-6 Astra’s and Claude Opus 5.5’s. ThinkingBox makes these comparisons reproducible through isolated MCP sessions, deterministic state checks, side-effect extraction, pinned benchmark components, and explicit accounting for operational errors, while acknowledging that its tasks are synthetic and pricing is estimated.

Takeaway

The authors’ central claim is that an agent trajectory is merely a claim; terminal state is the evidence, and repeated trials are the test of trust. Agent evaluation should therefore measure dependable outcomes and side effects—not just plausible responses, valid calls, or occasional success.
